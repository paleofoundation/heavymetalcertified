## The short answer

Which heavy-metal certification is the most comprehensive? On coverage &mdash; the number of metals that carry a published limit &mdash; the answer is narrow and, within its scope, checkable. As of August 2026, and on a documented competitive scan, Heavy Metal Tested &amp; Certified (HMTc) is the only third-party certification that sets a published limit for all eight priority metals: lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury, nickel, tin, aluminium, and chromium, measured as a ten-analyte panel. The nearest programs stop at four; NSF/ANSI 173 reaches five with hexavalent chromium added; and the pharmaceutical elemental-impurity standard ICH Q3D reaches seven but excludes aluminium and is a drug standard rather than a consumer-product seal.

HMTc certifies consumer packaged goods &mdash; foods, dietary supplements, and other product categories &mdash; against a published number, on a ten-analyte panel, in the form each product is sold in. This is a coverage claim, not a claim to sit below every government on every metal. Each HMTc limit defaults to the strictest existing maximum set by a credible government regulator for the product&ndash;analyte pair, converted to the product&rsquo;s native basis; the program states where its own threshold is tighter and why. What makes it comprehensive is how many metals it covers &mdash; not being lower than the law everywhere.

## Coverage, side by side

Every certification below screens the &ldquo;big four&rdquo; &mdash; lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury &mdash; the metals for which the FDA, the EU (Regulation 2023/915), and Codex already set maximums. The differences are entirely in the four metals beyond them: chromium, nickel, tin, and aluminium.

## Where the programs diverge

The big four are settled ground. What separates a four-metal mark from an eight-metal one is whether the certification measures the metals no conventional panel reaches.

- Chromium &mdash; among the other marks, only NSF/ANSI 173 (dietary supplements) adds hexavalent chromium, an IARC Group 1 carcinogen by ingestion. HMTc screens total chromium and speciates hexavalent chromium where a credible pathway exists. There is no general food maximum level for it.
- Nickel &mdash; not covered by the conventional four-metal marks. The EU set its first binding food nickel limits in 2024 (Regulation 2024/1987); HMTc carries a nickel limit on every certified product.
- Tin &mdash; not covered by the conventional four-metal marks. Codex (CXS 193) and the ANZ Food Standards Code limit tin as a packaging-integrity sentinel; HMTc carries it for the same reason.
- Aluminium &mdash; the universal gap. No food or supplement certification sets an aluminium action level, and even ICH Q3D &mdash; 24 elements, the broadest elemental-impurity standard for pharmaceuticals &mdash; deliberately excludes it. HMTc is the only certification in this comparison that publishes an aluminium limit.

## Why the gap matters

A certification that screens four metals cannot speak to the other four. A brand that carries a four-metal mark has no certified answer on nickel, tin, aluminium, or chromium &mdash; the metals whose limits governments are now writing. The [Big 8 briefing](/briefings/the-big-8) documents the competitive scan behind this page; [Four Metals Is No Longer Enough](/briefings/brief-7-four-vs-eight) traces the regulatory movement making the four-metal panel a shrinking floor. The full eight-metal panel, and the toxicology behind each metal&rsquo;s inclusion, is set out on the [analyte pages](/analytes); the numeric limits, per subcategory, live in the [standards register](/standards).
