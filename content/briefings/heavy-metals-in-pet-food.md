## Abstract

<p style="font-size:16.5px;line-height:1.78;color:#3f3a3a;margin:0 0 18px;max-width:72ch">A dog or a cat eats one manufactured food, from one recipe, every day for a decade or more. It is the closest thing in the household to an infant on formula: a sole-source diet, delivered to a small body, without the dietary variety that dilutes any single exposure. Yet the heavy-metal limits that govern that food are not the limits that govern food for people. Pet food is regulated as animal feed, and feed limits are written with a wide margin above the human-food maxima the same governments enforce. This briefing sets out what the published literature actually measures in commercial pet food, why each metal matters for a companion animal, and why the Heavy Metal Tested &amp; Certified program certifies pet food to the stricter human-food limit rather than the feed floor it is legally allowed to meet.</p>

## Two rulebooks for the same bowl

In law, pet food is feed, not food. In the European Union it is governed by Directive 2002/32 on undesirable substances in animal feed; in the United States by the Food and Drug Administration's Center for Veterinary Medicine and the model regulations of the Association of American Feed Control Officials; and in the national instruments of exporting countries. Those frameworks set maximum levels for the same metals human-food law addresses, but at a different height. Directive 2002/32 permits a complete pet feed to contain lead up to 5,000 &micro;g/kg, cadmium up to 2,000 &micro;g/kg, and total arsenic up to 2,000 &micro;g/kg on a 12-percent-moisture basis [6]. China's Hygienical Standard for Pet Feed sets lead at 5, cadmium at 2, chromium at 5, mercury at 0.3, and arsenic at 10 mg/kg [2] — the same lax band, expressed per kilogram. Japan's pet-food standard sits in the same range.

The human-food maxima the same governments enforce are far lower. The European Union limits cadmium in meat to 50 &micro;g/kg and in pulses to 40 &micro;g/kg; Israel limits inorganic arsenic in meat to 20 &micro;g/kg and lead in dairy to 20 &micro;g/kg; the strictest national limits for lead in muscle meat sit near 100 &micro;g/kg [7]. Between the feed ceiling a pet food may legally meet and the food ceiling its human-grade equivalent must meet lies a gap of ten to fifty times, and nothing in feed law asks a brand to close it.

Two of these frameworks do not set numeric heavy-metal limits at all. AAFCO's model regulations and the National Research Council's nutrient guidelines are silent on chromium, nickel, and aluminium in pet food; a survey of forty-nine United States dry dog foods noted the absence of any AAFCO or NRC standard for these elements as an open regulatory gap [4]. Where the rulebook is silent, the only limit is the one a certifier chooses to impose.

## What the literature finds in the bowl

The gap would be academic if commercial pet food were uniformly clean. The published record says otherwise, across every metal the program certifies.

Lead is the clearest case, and the most vivid example comes from raw diets built on game meat. A 2023 study analysed commercial raw minced pheasant dog food sold by United Kingdom online retailers and found a weighted mean lead concentration of 221 mg/kg dry weight in samples known or assumed to be free of visible shot, roughly thirty-four times the level expected from background contamination alone [1]. Between two-thirds and all of the packages of three raw pheasant products exceeded the European maximum level for animal muscle, while tinned pheasant and every chicken product tested at zero exceedances. X-ray imaging found radio-dense fragments, embedded lead shot, in three-quarters to all of the raw pheasant packages, and ten percent still contained shot at the point of analysis after macroscopic removal. The same study found that pheasant sold for human consumption exceeded the European limit in sixty-nine percent of samples, which is exactly the point: a game-meat ingredient too contaminated for a person is not thereby fit for a dog.

Arsenic and lead recur in broad market surveys. An analysis of 196 commercial pet-food products from Lebanon and the United Arab Emirates, measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, found lead and arsenic exceeding regulatory safety thresholds in a portion of the sampled foods, with the contamination distributed across both dry and wet formats [3]. A separate assessment of 93 imported cat and dog foods sold in Chinese supermarkets measured lead, cadmium, chromium, mercury, and arsenic in every sample and ran an acute hazard-index calculation for cats and dogs; the study reported measurable burdens of all five metals and higher mean lead and cadmium in cat food than in dog food [2].

Cadmium tracks the animal fraction. Organ meat, bone meal, and marine ingredients concentrate cadmium, and the surveys reflect it: the Lebanon and UAE study measured mean cadmium of 251 &micro;g/kg in cat food against 112 &micro;g/kg in dog food, a difference consistent with the higher marine and organ content of feline diets [3]. Both figures sit at or above the strictest human-food maximum for meat.

Mercury is the metal that most distinguishes cat food. Fish and marine protein are staples of feline diets, and methylmercury biomagnifies up the aquatic food chain in a way it never does in terrestrial ingredients. The Chinese survey measured mercury across cat and dog foods against a pet-feed ceiling of 0.3 mg/kg [2]; the human-food limit for the fish those diets are built from is lower still, and it is the human limit, split into total and methylmercury, that a fish-based diet should answer to.

Aluminium, nickel, and chromium are the metals the rulebook forgets. The survey of forty-nine United States dry dog foods found aluminium above the average human daily intake on a per-calorie basis in every product tested, and chromium and molybdenum above human intake as well, against no governing standard for any of them [4]. Finished treats carry the same elements: a study of nutraceutical dog snacks measured cadmium, lead, chromium, and nickel in extruded and dried prototypes [5], the product class a pet most often receives outside its measured daily ration.

## Why it lands harder on a dog or a cat

Three features of companion-animal feeding turn a modest concentration into a meaningful dose. The diet is sole-source and lifelong, so there is no dietary variety to average an exposure down, and the exposure runs for the whole of a shorter life. Body mass is small, so a fixed concentration delivers a larger dose per kilogram than the same food would deliver to a person. And the toxic metals do not clear; lead deposits in bone, cadmium accumulates in the kidney over years, and methylmercury crosses into the nervous system. A pet is, in exposure terms, a small body on a formula diet, which is the population human-food law protects most stringently, not least.

The species differ in ways the diet amplifies. Cats eat more fish and organ meat, which is where mercury and cadmium concentrate. Dogs on raw or game-based diets take on the lead burden of the meat's own contamination, as the pheasant data show. Neither animal chooses its food, varies it, or can be told to eat less of a bad lot; the entire margin of safety is set upstream, by whoever decides what the food is allowed to contain.

## The case for a human-grade standard

The Heavy Metal Tested &amp; Certified program resolves the two-rulebook problem in one direction only. For every pet-food subcategory and every metal, the standard is not the feed limit but the strictest maximum level a credible government sets for the nearest human food, adopted as a voluntary over-compliance and stated in the product's own basis. A dry complete diet is held to the strictest human limits for the cereals, pulses, and starchy tubers it is built from; a canned or raw diet to the human limits for meat; a fish-based diet to the human seafood limits, mercury split into both forms; a milk replacer to the human limits for dairy. The metals no government limits in any food, aluminium among them, are not left blank: they are measured on every lot as controls, and hexavalent chromium is treated as the measurement artifact it is, bounded by total chromium.

The result is a standard that reads, cell by cell, as a deliberate step down from the feed floor. Where feed law permits 5,000 &micro;g/kg of lead in a complete diet, the human-grade limit for dry pet food is 100; where it permits 2,000 &micro;g/kg of cadmium, the human-grade limit is 40. Each of the ten subcategory standards discloses the feed floor beside the human number it is certified to, so the over-compliance is stated, not implied. That disclosure is the argument: a certified pet food is held to the maximum levels that govern food for people, and the gap it closes is written on the page.

None of this rests on novel toxicology or on limits invented for the purpose. Every certified number is a sovereign human-food maximum already in force somewhere in the world, applied to a pet product by choice. The literature baseline for pet-food occurrence and for each metal is maintained independently at the [Heavy Metal Index](https://heavymetalindex.com); this program applies those findings, and the two are kept editorially separate by design.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is pet food regulated for heavy metals at all?

Yes, but as animal feed rather than as food. The EU's Directive 2002/32, the FDA's Center for Veterinary Medicine, AAFCO's model regulations, and national pet-feed standards set maximum levels for several metals, generally far higher than the limits the same governments set for human food. Some metals, including chromium, nickel, and aluminium, are not addressed by AAFCO or NRC at all.

### What does &ldquo;human-grade&rdquo; heavy-metal certification mean here?

For each pet-food subcategory and metal, HMTc certifies to the strictest maximum level a credible government sets for the nearest human food, not the laxer feed limit. The feed floor is disclosed beside every certified value so the voluntary over-compliance is visible.

### Which metals matter most in pet food?

Lead in game-meat and raw diets, arsenic in grain- and rice-based diets, mercury in fish-based cat food, and cadmium in organ- and marine-heavy diets are the best-documented signals in the published surveys. Aluminium, nickel, and chromium recur in dry foods against no governing limit.

### Why are pets more exposed than people?

A companion animal eats one manufactured diet for its whole life, has a small body mass that raises the dose per kilogram, and cannot vary its food. Lead, cadmium, and methylmercury accumulate rather than clear, so a lifelong sole-source diet concentrates the exposure.
