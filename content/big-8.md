Nearly every heavy-metal claim on a food label rests on four metals: lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury. That number is an inheritance from a single 2021 congressional report, not a toxicological conclusion, and in the years since the regulatory floor has moved to eight. Heavy Metal Tested &amp; Certified certifies against all eight, tested as ten analytes. What follows is why that difference is the one a brand's quality lead, and its lawyer, will care about.

## The coverage gap

A certification's analyte panel is a public statement of what a brand chose to look for. The table below is a documented competitive scan, current to August 2026, of what each mark actually sets a limit for.

HMTc is the only food certification that sets a published limit for all eight metals. The four-metal convention (California Assembly Bill 899, the FDA's Closer to Zero action levels, and the common purity seals) covers the original four; NSF/ANSI 173 adds hexavalent chromium to reach five [14]; ICH Q3D reaches seven but excludes aluminium, and is a pharmaceutical elemental-impurity standard rather than a food seal [15]. Aluminium is the universal gap: no other food or supplement certification sets a limit for it. Comprehensiveness here is about how many metals are covered, not about being lower than the law everywhere.

## Coverage is the commercial edge

The eight-metal panel is not a longer checklist for its own sake. It is the difference between a certificate that sits level with the law and one that trails several regulatory cycles behind it, and where these disputes are now decided, that difference has a price.

The Supreme Court's 2026 decision in *Hain Celestial Group v. Palmquist* made federal court materially harder for defendants to reach, so heavy-metal claims will increasingly be tried in state courts, before state juries, with the retailer anchored in the room. That forum rewards a simple, legible narrative, and a coverage gap is a simple, legible narrative. A plaintiff's expert needs no novel science to work a four-metal certificate: the European Union limits nickel, the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code limits aluminium and tin, EFSA and IARC have assessed all of them, and the certificate the brand paid for and displayed tested for none of them. A narrow panel is a documented boundary of what the brand chose to look for, and the narrower the panel, the sharper the line an expert can draw around it. Coverage that tracks the regulatory floor removes the line before it can be drawn. This is the reading a brand's counsel already applies; the page that lays it out is [After Palmquist](/briefings/after-palmquist), and the courtroom version is [The Evidence a Jury Will See](/briefings/defense-counsel-briefing).

## Where four came from

The four metals that define the category entered the public vocabulary through one document. On February 4, 2021, the Subcommittee on Economic and Consumer Policy of the U.S. House Committee on Oversight and Reform published a staff report finding that baby foods were tainted with dangerous levels of arsenic, lead, cadmium, and mercury [1]. The report tested for those four, named those four, and set the agenda around those four.

On its own terms the framing was sound: those four are genuine hazards with no safe threshold at the developmental endpoints that matter most in early childhood. What the report did not do, because it was not its purpose, was assert that only four metals warrant attention. Four is an artifact of which elements that one investigation measured, not a conclusion that the rest of the food supply is benign. The convention hardened anyway. California's Assembly Bill 899 requires baby-food manufacturers to test for and disclose the same four [5]; the FDA's Closer to Zero program works the same territory [6]; the voluntary seals settled on the same screen. A number chosen by one subcommittee became the industry's definition of thorough, and it was never revisited against what regulators did next.

## What regulators did next

While the four-metal convention was setting, the regulatory floor underneath it moved to eight.

The European Union consolidated its contaminant limits in Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915, carrying binding maximum levels for tin in canned foods, including 50 mg/kg for canned infant and young-child foods, one-quarter of the general canned-food cap [2]. In 2024 it went further and set its first binding food maximum levels for nickel, in Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/1987, a risk-management response to EFSA's finding that high dietary nickel exposure exceeds the tolerable level in infants, toddlers, and other children [3][7]. Those nickel limits adopt the law's own formulation split, 0.25 mg/kg for cow-milk-based infant formula powder and 0.40 mg/kg for soy-isolate formula powder, rather than a single blended value [3]. The pattern is not confined to Europe: the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code sets a maximum level for aluminium in infant formula products and, at Schedule 19, limits for tin in canned foods [4].

No single instrument regulates all eight in one place, and that is the point rather than a weakness in it. Different jurisdictions have limited different metals as their science and priorities allowed: the EU leads on nickel and tin, FSANZ on aluminium, the FDA on lead. Read together, the enforceable floor already spans eight metals. A four-metal mark sits at the 2021 convention, not at that floor.

## The case for each addition

Each of the four additions is anchored to a government that already limits or lists it. The tier and the honest reading of the toxicology are stated with each, because a claim a program cannot defend under scrutiny is worth less than the coverage it buys.

The original four are not diminished by the additions. They remain the Tier&nbsp;1 zero-tolerance toxics, each anchored in an EFSA opinion or an IARC Group&nbsp;1 classification [8][13], tested exactly as a four-metal program would test them. The additions extend the panel; they do not dilute it.

## Eight metals, ten analytes

HMTc certifies against eight metals and resolves them to ten analytes, because species drive both toxicity and law. Total arsenic is accompanied by speciated inorganic arsenic; total mercury by methylmercury; and chromium is screened as total chromium with hexavalent chromium determined on trigger. The distinction is not academic: the inorganic form of arsenic and the methyl form of mercury are the toxicologically governing species and carry their own regulatory limits, so a program that reported only the totals would be measuring the wrong thing precisely.

The panel does not change by product. Every certified product is tested on the full panel in every subcategory, rather than a metal being dropped because a category is assumed not to carry it. Assumption is what a coverage gap is made of. The per-analyte toxicology, the government reference values, and the occurrence data for each metal are set out on [the analyte panel](/analytes).

## Why eight, and not twelve

A longer list is not automatically a better one, and the boundary matters as much as the additions. HMTc uses an inclusion test rather than a round number: a metal enters the panel only when a credible government already limits or lists it in food. Every metal past the 2021 four clears that test. Nickel and tin are binding maximum-level lookups; aluminium is a disclosed read-across from the single jurisdiction that caps it; hexavalent chromium is regulator-listed and assessed, carried on the species discipline its toxicology demands. Eight is not the end of that list; it is the current state of it, and the apparatus that produced eight is built to add the ninth when the evidence and the law call for it. That is what keeps the scope principled rather than promotional, and it is the answer to the reasonable question of where a program decides to stop.

## What the mark claims — and what it doesn't

The comprehensiveness claim is narrow and, within its scope, strong: on a documented competitive scan, HMTc is the only food certification that sets a published limit for all eight metals, and aluminium is the universal gap no other program touches. What the program does not claim is to sit below every government on every metal. Its method is to adopt the strictest existing limit set by a credible government regulator for each product-analyte pair, converted to the product's native basis, tightened by the literature only where the toxics beat that floor, and to state where its own threshold is tighter and why. It is sometimes stricter than a government limit, never looser. The mark also does not assert that a product is safe: certification is a statement about a measurement against a published threshold, not a health verdict. A program claiming otherwise would be making a claim its evidence cannot support, and the whole value of the mark rests on it not doing that.
