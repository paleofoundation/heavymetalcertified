## In brief

Mercury is limited in food because of one form — methylmercury — that the body handles in the worst possible way for a developing brain. It crosses the placenta and the blood-brain barrier, accumulates in neural tissue, and has no established safe threshold for the developmental effects that anchor its regulation. Unlike most contaminants, it is concentrated in a single part of the food supply: large predatory fish, up the marine food chain. Cooking does not reduce it. That makes the certification question tractable and specific — measure total mercury, speciate to methylmercury, and hold the fish categories that carry it to the strictest verified number.

## The health basis for limiting mercury

The hazard that anchors dietary mercury regulation is methylmercury's developmental neurotoxicity. It crosses the blood-brain barrier and the placenta and accumulates in developing neural tissue, so the fetus is exposed at the most sensitive stage of brain formation. The evidence base is two long birth-cohort studies — the Faroe Islands and Seychelles studies — and the major bodies read it into converging but not identical guidance: EFSA set a tolerable weekly intake for methylmercury of 1.3 µg/kg body weight in 2012, JECFA a provisional tolerable weekly intake of 1.6 µg/kg body weight in 2004, and the US EPA the most conservative value, a reference dose of 0.1 µg/kg body weight per day. ATSDR's 2024 profile sets a chronic oral minimal risk level for methylmercury of 0.1 µg/kg/day.

Because it is the organic, methylated form that carries the developmental risk, the analyte is measured as total mercury and speciated to methylmercury where the food warrants it. The full agency record and the cohort evidence are on [the mercury analyte reference](/analytes/mercury).

## Who the exposure reaches

The most vulnerable population is the fetus, and after birth the young child, because the critical endpoint is neurodevelopment during the prenatal and early-postnatal windows. That is why dietary advice on fish is targeted at pregnancy and early childhood, and why a certification limit on the fish categories that carry methylmercury protects exactly the eaters — expectant mothers and young children — whose exposure has the largest and most permanent consequences. The exposure is also unevenly distributed: communities that rely on frequent fish consumption can carry a far higher body burden than a national average implies.

## Where mercury shows up in food

Methylmercury is unusual among food contaminants in being concentrated almost entirely in one place: fish, and specifically the large predatory species at the top of the marine food chain, where it biomagnifies with age and trophic level. Shark, swordfish, king mackerel, marlin, bigeye tuna and tilefish carry the highest and most consistent loads; most other foods contribute little. One practical consequence matters for certification — cooking does not reduce methylmercury, so the concentration on the plate is the concentration in the raw fish.

The per-category occurrence figures and the derivation behind each limit are on the [standards register](/standards); the complete occurrence corpus and the speciation evidence are on [the Heavy Metal Index](https://heavymetalindex.com/metals/mercury).

## Why a certification limit is warranted

The concentration of the exposure in a few high-trophic species is what makes a certification limit effective: a limit does not have to touch most of the diet to remove most of the risk, because most of the methylmercury is in a small set of fish categories. Governments set maximum levels — the EU and Codex cap mercury in fish, with tighter figures for species destined for infant and child foods — but a maximum level is a ceiling, not a verified statement about a specific lot.

HMTc classifies methylmercury as a priority toxic, speciates total mercury to methylmercury where a total figure could overstate the load, and sets its certification limit to the lower of the strictest existing government maximum level and the certified-market occurrence value, checked lot by lot. A regulatory hard-stop blocks certification of any product over an applicable legal limit. How the limit is derived is set out under [how limits are derived](/program/structure).

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is mercury in fish a concern for pregnancy?

The methylmercury in fish crosses the placenta and accumulates in the developing brain, and there is no established safe threshold for the developmental effects that anchor its regulation. The fetus is exposed at the most sensitive stage of brain formation, which is why fish advice is targeted at pregnancy and early childhood.

### Does cooking remove mercury from fish?

No. Methylmercury is bound in the fish tissue and is not reduced by cooking, so the concentration on the plate is essentially the concentration in the raw fish. Trimming and cooking methods that lower some contaminants do not lower methylmercury.

### Which fish are highest in mercury?

The large predatory species at the top of the marine food chain: shark, swordfish, king mackerel, marlin, bigeye tuna and tilefish. Methylmercury biomagnifies with age and trophic level, so long-lived predators carry the most; smaller, shorter-lived species carry much less.

### What is the difference between total mercury and methylmercury?

Total mercury includes inorganic mercury and the organic methylmercury form. In fish, most of the mercury is methylmercury, which carries the developmental risk. HMTc measures total mercury and speciates to methylmercury where a total figure could overstate the toxic load.

### How does HMTc set its mercury limit?

HMTc treats methylmercury as a priority toxic, speciates total mercury to methylmercury where needed, and sets its certification limit to the lower of the strictest existing government maximum level and the certified-market occurrence value, verified by lot testing.
