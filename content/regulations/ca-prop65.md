<section id="quick" style="margin-bottom:52px"><h2 style="font-family:Lora,serif;font-weight:400;font-size:30px;line-height:1.2;color:#2b1f1f;border-bottom:1px solid #e8e1db;padding-bottom:10px;margin:0 0 20px">Quick read</h2><p style="font-size:16.5px;line-height:1.78;color:#3f3a3a;margin:0 0 18px;max-width:72ch">California Proposition 65 is a right-to-know warning statute, not a food-safety standard. It requires the state to list chemicals known to cause cancer or reproductive harm, and requires a warning before a business exposes anyone to a listed chemical above a safe-harbour level. Five of the eight HMTc metals are listed &mdash; lead, cadmium, inorganic arsenic, hexavalent chromium, and <a href="/analytes/nickel" style="color:#1c43ad">nickel</a> &mdash; and yet Proposition 65 sets no maximum level for any of them in food. <a href="/analytes/nickel" style="color:#1c43ad">Nickel</a> is the sharpest case: California listed it for developmental and male-reproductive harm in 2018 and then adopted no oral safe-harbour at all, so no California number limits dietary nickel. This page records the listings and the gap they leave; it does not set any HMTc threshold. The narrative version is the briefing <a href="/briefings/the-prop-65-gap" style="color:#1c43ad">Named as a Hazard, Left Unlimited</a>.</p></section>
<section id="instrument" style="margin-bottom:52px"><h2 style="font-family:Lora,serif;font-weight:400;font-size:30px;line-height:1.2;color:#2b1f1f;border-bottom:1px solid #e8e1db;padding-bottom:10px;margin:0 0 20px">Mandate &amp; scope</h2><p style="font-size:16.5px;line-height:1.78;color:#3f3a3a;margin:0 0 18px;max-width:72ch">California's Proposition 65 (the Safe Drinking Water and Toxic Enforcement Act of 1986, Cal. Health &amp; Safety Code &sect;25249.5 et seq.) is administered by the Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment (OEHHA). It maintains the list of chemicals known to the state to cause cancer or reproductive toxicity, and derives the exposure levels below which a business is exempt from the warning requirement: a No Significant Risk Level (NSRL) for carcinogens and a Maximum Allowable Dose Level (MADL) for reproductive toxicants. A listing establishes a warning obligation; a safe-harbour level is a warning trigger measured in micrograms of intake per day, derived through a fixed formula. Neither is a maximum permitted concentration in food. Proposition 65 is enforced through litigation, including by private plaintiffs, which makes it commercially consequential out of proportion to California's size &mdash; but it governs exposure and warnings, never the concentration a food may contain.</p><div style="border:1px solid #e8e1db;background:#fffdfb;padding:16px 18px;display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:space-between;gap:18px;flex-wrap:wrap"><div style="min-width:0"><div style="font-family:Lora,serif;font-size:17px;line-height:1.35;color:#2b1f1f">The Proposition 65 List, official text</div><div style="font-size:12.5px;line-height:1.6;color:#a9a1a1;margin-top:3px">Published by OEHHA; the listing dates and safe-harbour levels below are transcribed from it and the OEHHA safe-harbour tables.</div></div><a href="https://oehha.ca.gov/proposition-65/proposition-65-list" style="font-family:'IBM Plex Mono',monospace;font-size:10.5px;letter-spacing:.08em;text-transform:uppercase;color:#fff;background:#9f2727;border:1px solid #9f2727;border-radius:2px;padding:8px 15px;text-decoration:none;white-space:nowrap" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Primary source &nearr;</a></div></section>

<section id="nickel" style="margin-bottom:52px"><h2 style="font-family:Lora,serif;font-weight:400;font-size:30px;line-height:1.2;color:#2b1f1f;border-bottom:1px solid #e8e1db;padding-bottom:10px;margin:0 0 20px">The nickel gap</h2><p style="font-size:16.5px;line-height:1.78;color:#3f3a3a;margin:0 0 18px;max-width:72ch">Nickel is the case that shows the statute's shape most clearly. On 26 October 2018 OEHHA added <a href="/analytes/nickel" style="color:#1c43ad">nickel</a> (soluble compounds) to the Proposition 65 list as a developmental and male-reproductive toxicant, after its own hazard-identification review found reduced offspring viability and body weight to be the most consistent effects of prenatal nickel exposure. California, in other words, wrote into law that dietary nickel can harm a developing child. And then it set no number. Nickel's only Proposition 65 safe-harbours are two inhalation cancer levels &mdash; for nickel refinery dust and nickel subsulfide &mdash; aimed at occupational dust, not diet. There is no oral MADL and no oral NSRL for nickel or nickel compounds as a class. The hazard is named; the dietary limit is absent.</p><div style="border-left:3px solid #9f2727;background:#fdf4f3;padding:18px 22px;margin:0 0 22px"><p style="font-size:15.5px;line-height:1.75;color:#5a4141;margin:0">California named nickel a reproductive toxicant and declined to set a dietary limit for it. <section id="gap" style="margin-bottom:52px"><h2 style="font-family:Lora,serif;font-weight:400;font-size:30px;line-height:1.2;color:#2b1f1f;border-bottom:1px solid #e8e1db;padding-bottom:10px;margin:0 0 20px">The pattern across the five</h2><p style="font-size:15.5px;line-height:1.75;color:#3f3a3a;margin:0 0 16px;max-width:84ch">Nickel is the sharpest instance of a pattern that holds across all five listings. California has formally judged each metal to cause cancer or reproductive harm. For lead, cadmium, and hexavalent chromium it went further and set an oral Maximum Allowable Dose Level that operates as a de-facto labelling threshold for consumer products. For inorganic arsenic it set a cancer No Significant Risk Level but no oral MADL, and for nickel it set no oral safe-harbour at all. And in every case the safe-harbour, where one exists, is a warning trigger measured in micrograms per day of intake &mdash; not a maximum concentration in the food itself. Proposition 65 never sets a food maximum level, because that is not what the statute does.</p><p style="font-size:15.5px;line-height:1.75;color:#3f3a3a;margin:0;max-width:84ch">The consequence is a gap. For these metals the operative dietary reference values are the European Food Safety Authority's tolerable intakes, and the binding maximum levels are the <a href="/regulations/eu-2023-915" style="color:#1c43ad">European Union's</a>; the United States sets no binding food maximum level for any of them, and its only federal numbers are the <a href="/regulations/us-fda-c2z" style="color:#1c43ad">FDA's action levels</a> for lead and inorganic arsenic, issued as guidance. California, the one US jurisdiction to have named the hazard in law, stops at a warning and leaves the concentration question unanswered.</p></section>

<section id="hmtc" style="margin-bottom:20px"><h2 style="font-family:Lora,serif;font-weight:400;font-size:30px;line-height:1.2;color:#2b1f1f;border-bottom:1px solid #e8e1db;padding-bottom:10px;margin:0 0 20px">How this interacts with HMTc</h2><p style="font-size:15.5px;line-height:1.75;color:#3f3a3a;margin:0;max-width:84ch">This gap is the space the certification program occupies. Where a government has named a heavy-metal hazard but set no dietary limit, an HMTc standard adopts the <em>strictest</em> maximum level any credible government has set for that product and analyte worldwide, in the product's native basis, and certifies each lot against it. This page records the sovereign listings and the dietary threshold California did not set; it does not set or justify any HMTc value. The certification limits, and the government maximum levels each one cites, live on the <a href="/standards" style="color:#1c43ad">standards register</a>; the full toxicological and occurrence record for each metal is on the <a href="/analytes" style="color:#1c43ad">analyte pages</a>; and the reference instruments that do set food limits are the rest of the <a href="/regulations" style="color:#1c43ad">regulation references</a>.</p></section>
