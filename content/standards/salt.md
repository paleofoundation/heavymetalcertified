Occurrence evidence cannot fill that gap. <a href="/analytes/cadmium" style="color:#1c43ad;text-decoration:none">Why cadmium is limited →</a></p></div><div style="margin:0 0 26px;padding:2px 0 2px 16px;border-left:2px solid #e4dcd5"><div style="font-family:'IBM Plex Mono',monospace;font-size:11px;letter-spacing:.07em;text-transform:uppercase;color:#9a8f8f;margin:0 0 8px">Aluminium</div><p style="font-family:'IBM Plex Mono',monospace;font-size:11.5px;color:#b7aeae;margin:0">No citable primary occurrence measurement is routed to this cell in the contextual corpus.</p><p style="font-size:14.5px;line-height:1.7;color:#3f3a3a;max-width:84ch;margin:0 0 10px">HMTc publishes no standalone aluminium limit for this category. Aluminium in salt derives from permitted anti-caking additives, not contamination. In the US, sodium aluminosilicate is GRAS as an anti-caking agent in table salt up to 2% (21 CFR 182.2727). In the EU most aluminium-silicate anti-caking additives were removed for consumer protection by Reg (EU) 380/2012 (E556/E558/E559 banned from 2014; E554/E555 restricted to non-salt uses), so EU table salt carries little additive Al. Dietary aluminium is managed by intake against the EFSA TWI (1 mg/kg bw/week) and JECFA PTWI (2 mg/kg bw/week) with additive use-level controls, not by per-food contaminant MLs; oral bioavailability from food is ~0.1-0.3%. No credible regulator sets an aluminium contaminant ML for salt. The single pooled datum (n=1, 164 mg/kg) cannot distinguish additive from natural background. <a href="/analytes/aluminum" style="color:#1c43ad;text-decoration:none">Why aluminium is limited →</a></p></div></section>
