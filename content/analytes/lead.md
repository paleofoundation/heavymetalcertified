<section style="margin-bottom:40px"><h1 style="font-family:Lora,serif;font-weight:400;font-size:38px;line-height:1.1;color:#2b1f1f;margin:0 0 8px">Lead</h1><div style="font-family:'IBM Plex Mono',monospace;font-size:12px;letter-spacing:.04em;color:#b7aeae;margin:0 0 18px">82 &middot; Pb &middot; Total elemental lead</div><p style="font-size:17px;line-height:1.7;color:#3f3a3a;margin:0;max-width:80ch">Lead is limited because its developmental neurotoxicity in young children has no demonstrated threshold — no agency can identify an intake or blood-lead level below which harm does not occur — so the governing frame is to minimize exposure as far as practicable, not to stay below a "safe" dose.</p></section>

<section id="toxicology" style="margin-bottom:52px"><h2 style="font-family:Lora,serif;font-weight:400;font-size:30px;line-height:1.2;color:#2b1f1f;border-bottom:1px solid #e8e1db;padding-bottom:10px;margin:0 0 20px">Toxicological basis</h2><p style="font-size:15.5px;line-height:1.75;color:#3f3a3a;margin:0 0 16px;max-width:84ch">Lead is primarily a developmental neurotoxicant. EFSA (2010) identified developmental neurotoxicity in young children as the most sensitive critical endpoint, deriving a BMDL01 of 12 µg/L blood lead (dietary equivalent 0.50 µg/kg body weight per day), with cognitive deficits, behavioural change, and reduced IQ documented in cohort studies below that benchmark — the empirical basis for the "no safe level" position. Children are at materially higher risk than adults: they absorb 30–50% of ingested lead (versus 5–15% in adults), retain more of it, and are exposed during the developmental window for brain maturation. Lead accumulates in bone (half-life 10–30 years; roughly 90% of adult body burden) and mobilizes during pregnancy and lactation, delivering endogenous exposure to the fetus and nursing infant decades after intake. Secondary adult endpoints include cardiovascular effects (systolic blood pressure, BMDL01 36 µg/L) and renal effects (chronic kidney disease, BMDL10 15 µg/L). Developmental neurotoxicity remains the governing endpoint; the cardiovascular and renal reference points are supporting adult endpoints, not the basis of the limit.</p></section>
<section id="regulation" style="margin-bottom:52px"><h2 style="font-family:Lora,serif;font-weight:400;font-size:30px;line-height:1.2;color:#2b1f1f;border-bottom:1px solid #e8e1db;padding-bottom:10px;margin:0 0 20px">How it is regulated</h2><p style="font-size:15.5px;line-height:1.75;color:#3f3a3a;margin:0 0 16px;max-width:84ch">No regulator has set a health-based tolerable intake for lead — JECFA withdrew its PTWI, EFSA reports BMDLs without a TWI, EPA IRIS derives no oral RfD, and ATSDR sets no MRLs — so the maximum levels in force are ALARA/technology-based ceilings rather than safety thresholds derived from a reference dose. For infant and child foods the strictest in-force government maximum level governs each product: Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/915 sets 20 µg/kg for baby food and processed cereal-based food and for formula powder, and 10 µg/kg for liquid formula, on an as-placed-on-market basis (<a href="/regulations/eu-2023-915" style="color:#1c43ad">/regulations/eu-2023-915</a>); Codex CXS 193 and FDA Closer to Zero sit near these values, with FDA's nonbinding baby-food action levels at 10 ppb (fruits, most vegetables, mixtures), 20 ppb (single-ingredient root vegetables), and 20 ppb (dry infant cereals) (<a href="/regulations/us-fda-c2z" style="color:#1c43ad">/regulations/us-fda-c2z</a>). Where no government regulates the exact product, the applicable value is read across from the nearest analogous government limit. The literature-native derivation and the full cross-jurisdiction table are maintained independently at the Index (<a href="https://heavymetalindex.com/metals/lead" style="color:#1c43ad">heavymetalindex.com/metals/lead</a>).</p></section>
<section id="hmtc" style="margin-bottom:52px"><h2 style="font-family:Lora,serif;font-weight:400;font-size:30px;line-height:1.2;color:#2b1f1f;border-bottom:1px solid #e8e1db;padding-bottom:10px;margin:0 0 20px">How the HMTc program treats it</h2><p style="font-size:15.5px;line-height:1.75;color:#3f3a3a;margin:0 0 16px;max-width:84ch">HMTc classifies lead as a Tier 1 (zero-tolerance) toxic — one of the four metals (Pb, Cd, iAs, MeHg/tHg) with no established safe threshold — so it carries no 150% transitional allowance (that applies only to the Tier 2 metals Ni, Sn, Al, Cr-VI) and triggers no speciation reflex (those apply to arsenic, mercury, and chromium, not lead). Under Method v2.0 the Tier 1 lead limit is set as min(occurrence percentile, strictest government maximum level); in the published Infant and Child Foods standards it resolves to the EU 20 µg/kg maximum converted to the product's native as-sold basis (for example, teething biscuits and finger snacks). The program's regulatory hard-stop blocks certification of any product that violates an actual legal lead limit regardless of HMTc status, so the certified ceiling can only ever sit at or below every applicable sovereign limit.</p></section>
